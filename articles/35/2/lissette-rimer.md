---
title:  "Turning Pain into Purpose: Author shares the story of a life ended far too soon --- and the lessons learned since"
author: Adam Minor
---
*Local author set to visit library to discuss story of son's suicide*

Patrick Wood had the world at his fingertips. The Pomfret native could
read at the age of 2. He excelled in academics and music, becoming a top
scholar at Pomfret Community School. He earned perfect SAT scores and
was the valedictorian of his graduating class of Pomfret School in 2001,
earning a scholarship to Stanford University, where he graduated in 2005
with honors and worked as a programmer for Siemens in Berlin, Germany.

But underneath all the success, Patrick was struggling.

"He was a miracle child," said his mother, Marie Lisette Rimer. "He did
everything you asked. He played well with others. He was a sterling
person. It's hard to put it into words. Everything about him was
\[impressive.\] You could not fault this person; he did his chores, he
ran upstairs after school and did his homework, didn't watch TV, and was
always working. He was filled with curiosity. He had this desire for
knowledge all the time. There was no stopping this person. He was
voracious about learning. ... but Patrick began to realize that the
sadness he was feeling wasn't normal. He wrote in one of his essays that
he would wake up not knowing if he was going to be OK that day or be
miserable that day. It was unpredictable. It was the unpredictability of
his own character that was scary --- that you couldn't even control your
own personality, even if you were doing everything right."

Less than a year after graduating from Stanford, in January 2006,
Patrick died by suicide.

The journey Rimer has been on since is chronicled in her 2024 book,
"Back from Suicide: Before and After the Essential Patrick." Rimer will
visit Eastford Public Library on Wednesday, Sept. 16, at 6 p.m. to
discuss the book, which Rimer admitted is a combination of Patrick's
story and an explanation of her "lack of understanding" about the mental
health issues that led to her son's death.

"I wanted people to know that you can think you are doing everything
right, but all of a sudden you get hit right in the face with a 2x4.
That's exactly what it was," she said, adding that both her mother and
father-in-law also died by suicide. "When my mother died, she used to
say it was family that made her drink and go into a manic depression. I
thought that was the secret to life --- have the best family you
possibly can, that will guarantee that things will turn out the best
they possibly can. I had no idea what I was talking about. I felt like
that was the safeguard."

Rimer said her thinking about suicide began to change once she began
doing research on mental health and depression, and began to see it as
"not logical, but biological."

"It's the interpersonal theory of suicide, and three components ---
isolation, being a burden on others, and having the capability, which
includes losing the fear of death, which I know now that I heard in
Patrick. I heard him lose that fear in his tone of voice. I felt when I
was writing the book that I had to go beyond Patrick's story, that I had
to offer readers something that was information they could take away,
and it was basically my journey about learning the biology of suicide
and depression and how Patrick got to the point where he wanted to die.
I could not reconcile the fact that this brilliant person who had the
world in the palm of his hand would want to do that, and I realized
after it happened that there was no other explanation other than that
suicide is a disease. It's the culmination of mental illness, and in his
case, major depression."

Another important aspect of Patrick's story revolved around his sexual
identity. Rimer reflected on the struggles he faced in his own mind,
grappling with his homosexuality, a struggle that would lead to
hospitalization at Stanford for suicidal ideation after a potential
relationship ended in disappointment.

"He called me up one night and said, 'I think I'm depressed,'" she said.
"I took him to a psychiatrist. He went on medication and regular
therapy. When he got to Stanford, we were 3,000 miles away. That's where
he wanted to be. I think he figured that California would be more
liberal. At the same time he was realizing his depression, he was
realizing he was gay. ... Depressed people cannot handle
disappointment."

Coming to terms with Patrick's death was an exercise in humility for
Rimer, who admitted that she was "naïve" and lacked understanding about
suicide, mental health, and depression. The decision to write a book
chronicling her journey after her son's death was born not only to pay
tribute to her son, but also to share what she has learned since.

"I felt like I let him die one time and I wasn't going to do it again,"
she said. "Despite all his success, I didn't understand that that is no
obstacle against depression. I didn't understand. I thought depression
was bad family or an abusive family. I thought good family could nurture
anybody out of anything. I had no understanding of the biology and the
genetics involved. No matter who you are, no matter if you are a failure
or the most successful person, it doesn't matter, because it's a medical
issue. ... I was so naïve about that. I figured you go away to college,
you figure it out, and there you go. I didn't understand. And that's
what the book is about."

Work on the book started in 2014, and Rimer said she spent nearly a
decade working on it.

"It was hard for me," she said about the writing process. "It's because
I am nothing close to a scientific person. I was a total ignoramus about
it, and every day I have to claw my way back into understanding how that
works. ... I needed people to know my ignorance about mental illness.
When it happened, I thought I missed everything. I realized I missed all
the signs, because I was relying on this brilliant person who was way
smarter than me, and I thought if anyone could push through it, it was
this brilliant person. I remember my father saying, 'Emotions are not
always solvable.' You don't solve them like a math equation. It doesn't
work. I hated writing it. I hated it. I felt like such a dope. But it
was right. I knew that I owed him that."

Since the book was released, Rimer has visited local establishments such
as the Putnam Public Library, the Coventry Public Library, and the
Windham County Chapter of the National Alliance on Mental Illness
(NAMI), and has enjoyed interacting with people who have shared her
experience.

"From the presentations I've given so far, the best part is people's
reactions," Rimer said. "The best part is sharing the stories. As I say,
you don't often get the opportunity to start talking about your personal
version of what happened. I love the fact that people take advantage of
this at these events. Oftentimes the Q&A is longer than the
presentation, and I love that. It's my favorite part. You can tell a
bunch of stats, but that doesn't work for me. What works for me is
learning from other people's stories. Maybe it's anecdotal, not
scientific, but I think the stories really help you understand how you
came back from suicide. And you can do it. I think that's the takeaway."
