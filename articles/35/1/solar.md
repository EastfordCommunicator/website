---
title: "Connecticut'S New Agrivoltaics Law: What It Means for Eastford's Agricultural Future"
author: Amelia Peloquin
---

Eastford is one of Connecticut's most rural communities. Its working
farms, forests, and open fields contribute not only to the local
economy, but also to the town's historic character and scenic
landscape. While solar projects and agriculture have seemed like
competing land uses in the past, a new solar law aims to bring them
together in an innovative way. Rather than viewing farming and solar
energy as competing land uses, the new agrivoltaics program enables
agricultural production and solar energy generation on the same land. As
Connecticut seeks to meet growing electricity demand while controlling
costs and preserving its agricultural heritage, the state's new solar
legislation marks an important policy shift.

The Connecticut General Assembly recently passed, and Gov. Ned Lamont
signed on June 4, H.B. 5340, An Act Concerning Renewable Power
Generation. The law establishes new programs, regulatory requirements,
and study mandates intended to advance Connecticut's renewable energy
goals. Although most of the media attention on this new law has focused
on plug-in solar systems (that is, individual solar panels that can
simply be plugged into an electrical outlet, and which are very
attractive to apartment dwellers and homeowners who just want a little
relief from their electricity bills), H.B. 5340 is much broader in
scope. Of particular interest to Eastford landowners is the new mandate
for the state to create an "agrivoltaics" program, which aims to help
working farmland generate solar energy in a way that fits in with its
traditional agricultural usage.

Because much of Eastford remains agricultural, any proposal for
utility-scale solar has the potential to generate significant public
interest. In Connecticut, rural residents often support renewable energy
conceptually, but also express concern about preserving productive
farmland and maintaining the town's rural character. Agrivoltaics offer
a potential compromise. Properly designed projects could provide farmers
with additional income while allowing agricultural production to
continue. This supplemental revenue may help family farms remain
financially viable during periods of fluctuating commodity prices and
increasing operating costs.

While the concept of agrivoltaics combines agriculture and solar
generation on the same property, it could work in different ways across
farms --- for example, by grazing sheep beneath solar panels or growing
certain crops around solar installations. For Eastford landowners with
active farmland, this could eventually create opportunities to generate
income from solar while maintaining agricultural use. The details still
need to be developed by state agencies, but it signals Connecticut's
interest in encouraging solar projects that preserve working farms
rather than converting farmland entirely to energy production.

For rural communities such as Eastford, where agriculture remains an
important part of the town's identity and economy, the legislation
offers both opportunities and challenges. While agrivoltaics presents
opportunities, successful implementation depends on careful planning.
Not every crop performs well beneath solar panels, and system designs
must allow adequate sunlight, equipment access, and drainage. Rural
communities will also need to evaluate visual impacts, construction
traffic, stormwater management, and compatibility with neighboring
properties. Maintaining genuine agricultural production --- not simply
placing a few animals on a solar site --- will be essential if
agrivoltaics are to achieve their intended purpose.

As the new law is implemented, Eastford's farmers, residents, and local
officials will play an important role in determining how agrivoltaics
will be part of the town's future. For Eastford, this could create an
opportunity to shape future development in ways that reflect local
values. By encouraging thoughtfully designed agrivoltaic projects that
protect prime farmland, the town can support clean energy without
sacrificing the agricultural landscape that has defined the community
for generations.

By extending solar programs, streamlining permitting, and exploring new
approaches such as agrivoltaics, the new solar law lays the groundwork
for Connecticut's renewable energy policies through 2035. It will be
worth watching how these changes affect homeowners, farmers, and
communities like Eastford in the years ahead.
